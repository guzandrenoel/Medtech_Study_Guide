I

Clearing / Dealcoholization

Clearing is the step where the dehydrating agent (usually alcohol) is removed from the tissue and replaced by a substance that makes the tissue receptive to the infiltrating medium. Alcohol and paraffin wax are both immiscible with each other — clearing is the necessary bridge.

Why clearing exists — four key ideas

01 / RECALL: DEHYDRATION

Dehydration removes water because water is immiscible with paraffin. But the dehydrating agent (alcohol) is also immiscible with wax — so you can't go straight from dehydration to infiltration.

02 / BRIDGE

The clearing agent is an intermediate solvent: miscible with alcohol on one side and with the infiltrating medium (or mounting agent) on the other.

03 / OPTICAL CLARITY

Clearing gives tissue transparency — most clearing agents have a high refractive index. Tissue clarity under the microscope is the visual cue that clearing is complete.

04 / VOLUME

Ideal ratio of clearing agent to tissue is 10:1.

Not all clearing agents make tissue transparent — clarity is the hallmark of most, not a strict requirement of the process itself.
I.A

Characteristics of an Ideal Clearing Agent

ShouldShould NOT
  • Be miscible with alcohol (removes the dehydrating agent easily)
  • Be miscible with, and easily removed by, melted paraffin wax / mounting agent
  • Make tissue transparent
  • Produce excessive shrinkage, hardening, or tissue damage
  • Dissolve out aniline dyes
  • Evaporate quickly in a water bath

1 · Xylene

THE LAB DEFAULT

Colorless, most commonly used clearing agent — used both for embedding and for mounting/coverslipping. Only suitable for tissue blocks <5 mm.

Two moments clearing happens:

  • Before infiltration/embedding — removes the dehydrating agent so tissue is receptive to wax.
  • Before mounting/coverslipping — removes residual alcohol left by certain stains.

Other uses: cytohistology, coverslipping, cleaning processors & microscope objectives, recycling slides.

Advantage
  • Cheap and relatively stable
  • Most rapid agent — clears urgent biopsies in 15–30 min
Disadvantage
  • Highly flammable
  • Tissue becomes hard/brittle if left >3 hours
  • Considerable hardening & shrinkage — avoid on nervous tissue, lymph nodes
  • Turns milky if tissue was incompletely dehydrated (fix: filter)
  • Irritates eyes, nose, respiratory tract; toxic/polluting

2 · Benzene

XYLENE SUBSTITUTE — RARELY USED
Advantage
  • Rapid — clears in 15–60 min
Disadvantage
  • Highly flammable
  • Considerable shrinkage if prolonged exposure
  • Extremely toxic: linked to cancer and aplastic anemia
Aplastic anemia in brief: bone marrow damage halts production of all blood cell types — RBC loss → anemia, WBC loss → immunodeficiency, platelet loss → bleeding risk. This is why benzene has fallen out of routine use.

3 · Toluene

SAFER, PRICIER

Less toxic than xylene and benzene; tolerates small amounts of residual water better. More expensive, so used less often despite being safer (Gregorios, 2017).

Advantage
  • Acts fairly rapidly (1–2 hrs)
  • Doesn't make tissue excessively hard/brittle
  • Not carcinogenic
Disadvantage
  • More expensive
  • Slower than xylene/benzene
  • Flammable; irritant fumes with prolonged exposure

4 · Chloroform

GENTLE AGENT

Slower-acting than xylene but causes far less brittleness.

Advantage
  • Good for large, tough tissues
  • Good for delicate tissues: nervous tissue, lymph nodes, embryos
  • Not inflammable
Disadvantage
  • Hepatotoxic with prolonged inhalation
  • Low volatility → slow wax impregnation afterward
  • Does not make tissue transparent

5 · Cedarwood Oil

GENTLE, OIL-BASED

Clears both paraffin and celloidin sections. Used for CNS tissue and cytological studies (smooth muscle, skin) because of its gentle nature.

Pattern to remember: oil-based clearing agents are generally gentle — less tissue damage, but slower and pricier than solvent-based ones.
Disadvantage
  • Extremely slow — 2–3 days (paraffin), 5–6 days (celloidin)
  • Slow wax impregnation (low volatility, high viscosity)
  • Becomes milky on prolonged storage (fix: filter before use)
  • Very expensive

6 · Aniline Oil

SPECIALTY / DELICATE SPECIMENS

Used for clearing embryos, insects, and very delicate specimens. Not part of routine processing; another gentle agent.

7 · Clove Oil

MINIMUM SHRINKAGE, HIGH MAINTENANCE
Disadvantage
  • Expensive
  • Degrades rapidly with reuse → becomes adulterated/contaminated
  • Slow, difficult wax impregnation
  • Tissue becomes brittle; removes aniline dyes
  • Dissolves celloidin — incompatible with that technique

8 · Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl₄)

CHEAPER CHLOROFORM

Used for clearing in embedding only (not mounting). Shares chloroform's properties but costs less.

Disadvantage
  • Considerable tissue hardening
  • Highly toxic on prolonged exposure
  • Same drawbacks as chloroform: slow impregnation, toxic, not transparent

9 · Tetrahydrofuran (THF)

UNIVERSAL AGENT

Capable of both dehydration and clearing, shortening total processing time. Non-toxic but has an offensive odor.

Caution: THF is toxic if ingested/inhaled. Vapors cause nausea, dizziness, headache, and anesthesia; it's an eye and skin irritant. Prolonged exposure (up to 6 months) may cause conjunctival irritation — process in a well-ventilated area (Gregorios, 2017).

10 · Dioxane

UNIVERSAL AGENT — USE WITH CAUTION

Also a universal (dehydrating + clearing) agent that significantly reduces processing time — tissues can be embedded in paraffin within 4 hours of fixation.

Disadvantage
  • Greater tissue shrinkage
  • High carcinogenic potential — dangerous to use
I.B.11

Other Xylene Substitutes

i · LIMONENE

Clear, colorless, citrus-scented — a natural oil extracted from lemon/citrus peel. A gentle clearing agent.

ii · COCONUT OIL

Advantages: non-hazardous, less expensive, less tissue shrinkage.
Disadvantage: may solidify at lower temperatures — clear inside an incubator to keep it molten.

Impregnation / Infiltration

The clearing agent is completely removed and replaced by a medium that fills all tissue cavities, giving the specimen a firm consistency suitable for sectioning.

Goal

Embedding vs. Infiltration

EmbeddingInfiltration
Provides external supportMedium penetrates tissue to provide internal support

The same medium is typically used for both steps.

Characteristics of an Ideal Impregnating Agent

  • Soluble in processing fluids — miscible with the clearing agent
  • Suitable for sectioning and ribboning, providing rigidity/support
  • Molten/liquid between 30–60°C to facilitate infiltration
  • Translucent / transparent and colorless
  • Chemically stable and inert
  • Homogenous
  • Capable of flattening after ribboning
  • Non-toxic, odorless, easy to handle, inexpensive

II.B.1

Paraffin Wax

A polycrystalline mixture of solid hydrocarbons produced during the refining of coal and mineral oils — the simplest, most common, and best infiltration medium.

Melting point
56–58°C
Use at
≥2°C above MP
Recommended temp
60°C

Used slightly above its melting point to stay fully liquid and less viscous for infiltration — too high a temperature can cook or damage the tissue. Section thickness with paraffin: 3–5 µm. Tissue blocks and unstained mounted sections may be stored indefinitely.

Paraffin Wax — Pros & Cons

Advantage
  • Very rapid process
  • Thin serial sections cut with ease
  • Indefinite storage of blocks/unstained sections
Disadvantage
  • Overheating causes brittleness, degrades wax quality
  • Considerable tissue shrinkage/hardening
  • Not recommended: delicate tissue (embryos, lymph nodes, mucous tissue)
  • Not recommended: dense/fatty/mineralized tissue (brain, bone)

Methods of Paraffin Impregnation

MethodKey parameterNotable trait
a) Manual processing≥4 wax changes, 15-min intervals; oven at 55–60°CSlowest method — extra changes compensate
b) Automatic processingWax bath ≥3°C above melting pointOnly 2–3 changes; built-in agitation speeds infiltration ("Dip and Dunk")
c) Vacuum embeddingWater-jacketed vacuum chamber, 2–4°C above MPReduces impregnation time 25–75%; fastest, gentlest result
Vacuum embedding is preferred for: urgent biopsies and delicate tissue — lung, brain, connective tissue, decalcified bone, eyes, spleen, central nervous system. It avoids high heat exposure, fully removes transitional solvents, and extends wax life by reducing solvent contamination.

Ester Wax — the shortcut it enables

Ester wax is insoluble in water but soluble in 95% ethanol and other clearing agents. Because the dehydrating agent (alcohol) can already dissolve it, clearing becomes unnecessary.

StepStatus
1. FixationContains water
2. DehydrationOrganic solvent (e.g., alcohol)
3. ClearingSkipped — already soluble in the dehydrating agent
4. InfiltrationWax

Carbowax (water-soluble wax) — the bigger shortcut

Soluble and miscible with water itself, so neither dehydration nor clearing is needed — fixation can lead straight into infiltration.

StepStatus
1. FixationContains water
2. DehydrationSkipped — wax is already soluble in water
3. ClearingSkipped — no organic solvent left to remove
4. InfiltrationWax

Applicable for enzyme histochemical studies, where preserving native chemistry matters more than speed of the older alcohol-based route.

II.B.2

Celloidin (Collodion)

A purified form of nitrocellulose, soluble in many solvents, available in thin/medium/thick concentrations. Requires sections of ≥10 µm — much thicker than paraffin's 3–5 µm.

Best suited for: specimens with large hollow cavities, hard/dense tissue, and large sections of whole embryos.

Celloidin — Pros & Cons

Advantage
  • Cuts very hard tissue without undue distortion
  • Avoids crumbling of dense tissue during sectioning
  • No heat required — dissolves in equal parts ether and alcohol
Disadvantage
  • Very slow impregnation
  • Thin sections and ribbons are difficult to prepare
  • Ether solvent is very flammable; the medium itself is volatile

Wet Celloidin — full procedure

StepAction
1Equal parts ether and alcohol, 12–24 hrs
2Increasing celloidin concentration: thin (2–4%) 5–7 days → medium (4–6%) 5–7 days → thick (8–12%) 3–5 days
3Transfer to fresh celloidin as the embedding medium

Gilson's mixture (used in the dry method) = equal parts chloroform and cedarwood oil — both clearing agents, added to make the tissue transparent.

Low-Viscosity Nitrocellulose

A lower-viscosity form of celloidin — can be used at higher concentrations while still penetrating tissue rapidly.

AdvantageDisadvantage

II.B.3

Gelatin

Used in producing sections of whole organs via a modified Gough-Wentworth technique combined with agar as a pre-embedding medium, and in frozen sectioning.

Like water-soluble wax, gelatin is miscible with water — dehydration and clearing are not needed; infiltration follows fixation directly.

Used for: histochemical and enzyme studies, delicate specimens, and frozen tissue sections — it's a gentle agent that won't induce much shrinkage or damage.

StepAction
1Fixation
210% gelatin + 1% phenol, 24 hrs
320% gelatin + 1% phenol, 12 hrs
4Fresh 20% gelatin + 1% phenol, then cool in a refrigerator
Why 1% phenol? It prevents/reduces microbial growth — gelatin is nutrient-rich and ideal for microbes. Concentration is capped at 1%, since higher levels can damage tissue.
II.B.4

Plastic (Resin)

Used for embedding in electron microscopy — plastic-infiltrated tissue is so well supported it allows ultra-thin sections to be cut. Also used for undecalcified bone. Typical thickness: 50–100 nm.

a) Epoxy

DisadvantageWhy it matters
HydrophobicResidual water in tissue makes infiltration ineffective
May reduce tissue antigenicityAntigens become undetectable — not usable for IHC
Possibly toxic—
Three epoxy types — viscosity vs. speed
TypeViscosityInfiltration rate
Bisphenol A (Araldite)Largest molecule, highest viscositySlowest of the three
Glycerol (Epon)Lower than AralditeSlightly higher
Cyclohexene Dioxide (Spurr)Lowest viscosityFastest
Epoxy resins remain the most common embedding media for semi-thin/ultrathin sections; acrylic resins are used instead where immunohistochemistry is required. Thicker resin sections (0.35–5 µm) can also be cut for light microscopy.

b) Polyester Plastics

Originally introduced for electron microscopy; once popular but found inferior to epoxides. Today its use is limited to electron microscopy.

c) Acrylic Plastics

Esters of acrylic or methacrylic acid, used extensively for light microscopy. Examples: Glycol Methacrylate (GMA), Methyl Methacrylate (MMA).
