I

Introduction to Staining

Staining renders different tissue components visible by conferring color variation — enabling optical differentiation and identification of cells and tissue structures that would otherwise be colorless and transparent after processing.

Key definitions

STAINING

A process that renders tissue components more visible through variation in color. End product: stained elements appear transparent and uniformly colored — no visible dye particles (no precipitates).

STAINS / DYES

Chemical substances used to achieve visible color contrast in the microscopic picture of a prepared tissue. Imparts characteristic color to usually transparent tissue.

Purpose of staining

  • Outlines tissue and cellular components
  • Identification of tissues
  • Establishes presence/absence of disease processes (congenital, inflammatory, degenerative, neoplastic)

2

3
RehydrationDescending concentrations of alcohol → distilled water. Required because most stains are water-based. Opposite of dehydration which is ascending.
II

Staining Mechanism

The principles of histological staining are based on two core alterations — contrast and color — to differentiate tissue components under the microscope.

Two types of staining based on target

Nuclear vs. Cytoplasmic staining — charge-based logic
TypeTargetCharge of targetDye charge neededReaction nameResult color
Nuclear Staining Nucleus (DNA) Acidic / Anionic / NegativePhosphate backbone of DNA Basic / Cationic / Positive Basophilic — dye-salt union Darker blue to purple
Cytoplasmic Staining (below pH 6) Cytoplasm proteins Net PositiveAmino group accepts H⁺ → ammonium ion Acidic / Anionic / Negativee.g., Eosin Acidophilic Reddish to orange
Cytoplasmic Staining (above pH 6) Cytoplasm proteins Net NegativeCarboxyl group donates H⁺ → carboxylate Basic / Cationic / Positive Basophilic Purplish to lighter blue
Memory trick — "opposites attract": Acidic tissue components attract basic (cationic) dyes. Basic tissue components attract acidic (anionic) dyes. The nucleus is acidic → stained by basic dyes (hematoxylin) → blue. Cytoplasm (at low pH) is positive → stained by acidic dyes (eosin) → pink.
III

Dyes — Chemistry & Components

All dyes are organic compounds — mostly coal tar derivatives centered on aromatic benzene rings (C₆H₆). Benzene alone is colorless (absorbs only UV at ~214 nm); it needs chemical modification to absorb visible light (400–700 nm).

Three integral components of a dye

–OH (hydroxyl) → anionic

Chromophore types

1 · QUINOID GROUP

Synthetic dyes

Two H atoms on benzene replaced by oxygen. May occur alone or as a whole structure.

Examples: Triphenylmethane → Basic Fuchsin · AZO → Congo Red · Xanthene → Eosin

2 · QUINONE-IMINE GROUP

Interbenzene bonds

Two benzene rings attached. Oxazines incorporate O₂ atoms; Thiazines incorporate sulfur atoms.

Examples: Oxazines → Cresyl Fast Violet · Thiazines → Toluidine Blue

Qualities of a successful staining

SPECIFICITY / SELECTIVITY

Stains specific target components — e.g., Periodic Acid-Schiff stains carbohydrates only.

SENSITIVITY

Detects even tiny/low concentrations of the target — e.g., Perl's Prussian Blue detects few iron granules, enabling early detection of disease.

VI

Differentiation / Decolorization

After regressive staining, differentiation selectively removes excess stain from the tissue using a weak acid or alcohol until desired intensity is achieved. Controlled by exact timing or microscopic examination.

Three types of differentiators

How each differentiator type works
TypeMechanismExamples
Acid DifferentiatorCombines with the metal in the dye-tissue complex, breaking that unionHCl, Acetic Acid
Oxidizing DifferentiatorOxidizes dye to a colorless substance (leukoform). Components with least dye bleached first.KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, Chromic Acid, Picric Acid, Potassium Ferricyanide
Mordant DifferentiatorMass action: binds dye to tissue while removing same dye from less-stained structures. Structures with most dye (nuclear chromatin) remain deeply stained.Ferric salts in iron hematoxylin

Four related agents — critical distinctions

Mordant vs. Accentuator vs. Accelerator vs. Trapping Agent
AgentWhat it doesForms lake with stain?Examples
MordantPolyvalent metal ion that bridges tissue and dye — forms a colored "lake" complex (tissue-mordant-dye). Insoluble in aqueous & alcoholic solvents. Increases affinity between dye and tissue.Yes — forms lakeAluminum (most common), Iron, Potassium, Ammonium alum
AccentuatorNo chemical union with tissue/dye. NOT a mordant. Hastens speed, intensity, and selectivity of the dye — but doesn't change the stain itself.NoKOH in Löffler's Methylene Blue, Phenol in Carbon Thionin, Glacial Acetic Acid, Aniline with Gentian Violet
AcceleratorIncreases the rate of staining action. NOT a mordant — does not form lakes. Used in metallic impregnation for the nervous system.NoChloral Hydrate
Trapping AgentHolds dye combination with tissue or bacteria — used to differentiate target/extracellular components (bacteria, fungi, parasites).N/ATannic Acid, Iodine
Exam trap: Only mordant forms a "lake." Accentuators and accelerators improve staining speed but are NOT mordants. Do not confuse them.
VII

Hematoxylin

A natural dye extracted from the heartwood of Haematoxylon campechianum (logwood). Its major oxidation product is Hematein — the actual dye responsible for color properties. Hematoxylin itself is a poor dye; it must be oxidized to hematein and paired with a mordant.

ETYMOLOGY

"Haimato" = blood · "Xylon" = wood (Greek)

SOURCE

Heartwood of Haematoxylon campechianum. Extracted with hot water, precipitated with urea.

HISTORY MILESTONES

1862 – Waldeyer established its use · 1864 – Böhmer + alum mordant · 1891 – Heidenhain iron-alum method · 1900s – Ehrlich overcame instability with glacial acetic acid.

Bluing / Blueing

Converts the initial soluble red color of hematoxylin-stained nuclei to an insoluble blue. The alkaline pH of the bluing solution causes the mordant-dye lake to reform permanently in the tissue.

SIX BLUING SOLUTIONS (all alkaline)
  • Ammonia water (routine H&E)
  • Warm/Distilled H₂O (40–50°C)
  • Lithium carbonate (carbonate ion = alkaline)
  • Bicarbonate (sodium bicarbonate = mild alkaline)
  • Potassium/Sodium Acetate
  • Scott's tap H₂O substitute (Na + K Bicarbonate + MgSO₄)

XI

Mounting & Coverslipping

Mounting is the placement of a coverslip over the stained tissue using a syrupy mounting medium to secure the section, protect it from physical damage and oxidation, and provide a refractive index match for clear microscopy.

Why refractive index matters

Refractive indices: Air = 1.0 · Water = 1.33 · Tissue = 1.33–1.34 · Glass = 1.52–1.53 · Mounting medium = 1.52
Without mounting: glass + air + tissue → light scatters → blurry image.
With mounting: glass + medium + tissue + coverslip all ≈ same RI → less scatter → sharp image. Target RI: n = 1.518 (glass & tissue must be ~1.53–1.54).

Characteristics of a good mounting medium

Should / should not properties
ShouldShould NOT
  • Have RI near glass (n = 1.518)
  • Be freely miscible with xylene & toluene
  • Set hard → permanent mounting
  • Quickly dry (causing cracks or artifactual granularity)
  • Dissolve out or fade tissue sections
  • Cause shrinkage or distortion of tissue
  • Bleach or affect staining
  • Cause changes in color or pH

XII

Self-Test Flashcards

Click each card to reveal the answer. Based on the reinforcement activity from the lecture.

1. What is the process that renders tissue components visible through variation in color?
Staining
2. T or F: Auxochromes alter the light resonance properties so that unequal absorption occurs when white light passes through.
FALSE — that is the function of Chromophores. Auxochromes are responsible for tissue binding via electrical charge.
3. What dyes came from nature and are found in plants/animals?
Natural dyes (e.g., Hematoxylin, Carmine, Orcein, Brazilin, Saffron, Cochineal)
4. What substance enhances the combination of the dye with the tissue?
Mordant — it forms an insoluble colored "lake" complex that bridges dye and tissue. The most common mordant is aluminum.
5. Who first firmly established the use of hematoxylin in histology?
Waldeyer (1862)
6. At the isoelectric point (IEP), what is the net algebraic charge of the molecule?
Zero — the molecule is called a zwitterion at IEP, with little affinity for charged stain molecules.
7. Which dyes are commonly used in intravital staining? (India Ink, Thionine, Carmine)
India Ink and Carmine (Both A & C). Thionine is supravital, not intravital.
8. What synthetic stain is used as a counterstain to the nuclear stain (hematoxylin)?
Eosin — specifically Eosin Y (most routinely used, water-soluble, imparts pink to pink-orange color)
9. T or F: In order to become a dye, hematin must be oxidized to hematein.
FALSE — it is the reverse. Hematoxylin is oxidized to form Hematein (the actual dye). Hematin is a different compound.
10. What is the main factor in progressive staining that determines depth of color?
Time — the longer the tissue is left in the stain, the more color it develops. Overstaining is prevented by monitoring time carefully.
11. What is the recommended pH for eosin staining and why?
Optimum pH 5.4–5.6 (slightly acidic). At this pH, cytoplasmic amino groups accept H⁺ to become positively charged, attracting the negatively charged eosin. Too basic → proteins less positive → weaker staining.
12. What does bluing (blueing) do in H&E staining, and why is it needed?
It converts the initial soluble red color of hematoxylin-stained nuclei to an insoluble blue. The alkaline bluing solution causes the mordant-dye lake to reform permanently in the tissue. Ammonia water is the standard bluing agent in routine H&E.
13. What is the refractive index target for mounting medium, and why does it matter?
n ≈ 1.518 (glass and tissue ≈ 1.53–1.54). Matching RI minimizes light scatter between glass, medium, tissue, and coverslip, producing a sharp microscopic image. Air (n=1) causes scattering and blur.
14. What are the three reasons for section failure during staining?
1. Dirty or oily slide (poor tissue mounting). 2. Faulty / incompletely ripened hematoxylin. 3. Residual paraffin, fixative, or decalcifying solution not thoroughly removed.
15. What is the difference between regressive and progressive staining?
Progressive: stain is applied until desired intensity is reached — no overstaining, time controls depth. Regressive: tissue is OVERSTAINED first, then excess dye is selectively removed (differentiated) until the desired result is achieved. Harris Hematoxylin can be used for both but is typically regressive in routine H&E.
