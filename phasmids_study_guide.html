Hookworms

Soil-transmitted helminths and blood-sucking nematodes that attach to the mucosa of the small intestine. No intermediate host. Eggs & larvae of both species are indistinguishable — reported simply as "hookworm."
🇵🇭 Necator americanus is the predominant human hookworm in the Philippines (90%).

Ovum (Identical in Both Species)
  • Size: 55–76 μm × 34–45 μm
  • Color: Colorless (not bile-stained)
  • Shape: Oval/elliptical (A. duo.) · Longer, elongated (N. amer.)
  • Shell: Transparent hyaline membrane, double-layered, no albumin layer
  • Contains segmented protoplasm, usually 2–8 blastomeres
  • Clear space between eggshell and vitelline membrane
Key memory tip: Feeding stage = mouth open (Rhabditiform). Infective stage = mouth closed (Filariform).
Hookworm ID is NOT based on eggs — eggs are indistinguishable from each other (and from Strongyloides at the larval stage, though hookworm rhabditiform has a longer buccal canal).
Larval Stages
Rhabditiform Larvae (L1 & L2) — Feeding Stage
  • Longer buccal cavity & canal
  • Smaller genital primordium
  • More tapered posteriorly
  • Hatches from egg within 24–48 hrs in suitable conditions
  • Transforms to L3 on day 5–8
vs. S. stercoralis: hookworm rhabditiform has longer buccal canal; S. stercoralis has a short one.
Filariform Larvae (L3) — Infective Stage
  • Mouth closed (non-feeding)
  • Esophagus elongated
  • Cuticle: transverse striations, ridges near tail
  • Tail: distinctly pointed posterior end
  • Larger than S. stercoralis filariform
  • Viable in soil for several weeks
Life Cycle
1
Skin penetrationL3 filariform larvae penetrate unprotected skin (usually feet). Produces maculopapular lesions, localized edema → Cutaneous Larva Migrans (CLM).
2
BloodstreamLarvae enter lymphatics and blood vessels.
3
Lung migrationBlood → Right heart → Lungs → Break out into alveoli → Bronchioles → Pharynx/Trachea → coughed up and swallowed.
4
Maturation in SIReach gastrointestinal tract → mature into adults in the small intestine. Attach to mucosa → abdominal pain, steatorrhea, diarrhea.
5
Copulation & egg productionAdult females lay 9,000–20,000 eggs/day.
6
Eggs passed in fecesReach morula/tadpole stage within hours. Hatch to L1 (Rhabditiform) within 24–48 hrs under ideal conditions (moist, shady, warm, sandy loam).
7
Molting (×2 in environment)L1 → L2 → L3 (infective). Ready to infect a new host.
Clinical Manifestations
Cutaneous
  • Maculopapular lesions
  • Localized erythema
  • Serpiginous / creeping eruption
  • Intense pruritus (itching)
Pulmonary
  • Bronchitis / pneumonitis
  • Swelling of bronchioles
  • Hemorrhage from intestinal walls
Systemic
  • Microcytic iron deficiency anemia (hypochromic)
  • Hypoalbuminemia
  • Eosinophilia
  • Protein malnutrition
  • Children: stunted growth, mental retardation
Laboratory Diagnosis & Treatment
Diagnostic Techniques
  • Direct Fecal Smear — cost-effective, good for heavy infections only (>400 epg)
  • Kato-Katz — quantitative; note rapid clearance (30–60 min) with glycerine
  • Concentration Method — zinc sulfate flotation OR formalin-ether/ethyl acetate
  • Harada-Mori culture — filter paper + tube; quantifies parasite load
  • ELISA / PCR
  • Fecal specimen >24 hrs at room temp → egg hatches → larvae visible
  • Heavily infected: Charcot-Leyden crystals in feces
  • Subnormal hemoglobin — very common
Treatment & Prevention
Single dose benzimidazole: Albendazole or Mebendazole. Note: drug resistance emerging.
  • Clean water & sanitation
  • Education
  • Regular deworming
  • Proper hygiene
  • Wear footwear

Humans are only accidental hosts. Larvae cannot complete their normal cycle in humans and migrate erratically → brain → eosinophilic meningitis.

Clinical Manifestations
Incubation: 6–15 days (up to several weeks). Generally good prognosis.
Symptoms
  • Acute, severe, intermittent occipital & bitemporal headache
  • Neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity)
  • Paresthesia (pins-and-needles)
  • Low-grade fever
  • Myopia / blurred vision
  • Myalgia
CSF Profile
  • Increased WBC (pleocytosis)
  • Eosinophilia >10% WBC (often up to 90%)
  • Charcot-Leyden crystals (bipyramidal, from eosinophil breakdown)
  • Occasional multinucleated giant cells
Severe / Untreated
  • Confusion, disorientation, coma (rare)
  • Intraocular hemorrhage, retinal detachment
Diagnosis & Treatment
Diagnosis
  • Travel history to endemic areas
  • Blood eosinophilia (peaks ~day 30)
  • CSF eosinophilia (90%)
  • CSF: young larvae / adult worms (definitive in 16/32 proven cases)
  • MRI: granulomas or meningeal enhancement
Treatment
  • Usually self-limiting (4–6 weeks)
  • Albendazole ± corticosteroids (used in China, Taiwan, Thailand)
  • Severe headache: spinal puncture, remove ~10 mL CSF
  • Ocular: surgical removal of larvae (eye removal if severe)
  • Caution: killing larvae in CNS may increase inflammation

Enterobius vermicularis

Most common helminth infection worldwide. Especially children in crowded/temperate environments. Eggs survive best on bedding, clothing, perianal skin in warm, humid conditions.

  • Use showers, not baths
  • Wash sleep clothes in hot soapy water
  • Isolate infected persons

  • Strict hygiene

Hookworms
What is the predominant human hookworm species in the Philippines, and what percentage of infections does it account for? +
Necator americanus — accounts for 90% of hookworm infections in the Philippines. It is also known as the New World hookworm or "American murderer" (from Latin necator = killer/murderer).
Compare A. duodenale and N. americanus: shape, buccal capsule, copulatory bursa rays, spicules, and female tail spine. +
A. duodenale: C-shaped · 4 hook-like (ventral) + 2 knob-like (dorsal) cutting teeth · 13 rays, dorsal ray tripartite · 2 separate, bristle-like, not fused spicules · Female tail spine present · More pathogenic.

N. americanus: S-shaped · 4 ventral semi-lunar cutting plates · 14 rays, dorsal ray bipartite · 2 spicules fused at tips (bifid/barb) · Female tail spine absent · Less pathogenic.
Why can you NOT identify hookworm species from eggs or larvae alone? What is the correct reporting practice? +
Eggs and rhabditiform/filariform larvae of A. duodenale and N. americanus are morphologically indistinguishable. Eggs are oval, colorless (not bile-stained), 55–76 × 34–45 μm, with a transparent hyaline double-layered shell (no albumin layer) and 2–8 blastomeres. When found in fecal specimens, they are reported simply as "hookworm" — species ID requires examination of adult worms.
How do you differentiate hookworm rhabditiform larvae from Strongyloides stercoralis rhabditiform larvae microscopically? +
Hookworm rhabditiform: Longer buccal cavity and canal · Smaller genital primordium · More tapered posteriorly · Larger overall size.

S. stercoralis rhabditiform: Short buccal canal (key differentiator) · More prominent genital primordium.

Memory tip: Hookworm = Long buccal canal; Strongyloides = Short.
Trace the hookworm life cycle from skin penetration to egg excretion. What clinical sign occurs at the skin entry site? +
L3 filariform penetrates unprotected skin → enters lymphatics & blood → right heart → lungs → breaks into alveoli → bronchioles → pharynx (coughed up & swallowed) → small intestine (matures into adult) → copulation → eggs pass in feces → hatch to L1 rhabditiform in soil → molt × 2 → L3 ready to re-infect.

Skin entry site: maculopapular lesions + localized edema = Cutaneous Larva Migrans (CLM) with intense pruritus (serpiginous, creeping eruption).
What type of anemia do hookworms cause and why? Name the classic systemic complications. +
Microcytic hypochromic iron-deficiency anemia — blood-sucking nematodes chronically extract blood from intestinal mucosa; loss of iron leads to low hemoglobin; RBCs are smaller than normal and their cytoplasm loses central pallor (hypochromic).

Other complications: Hypoalbuminemia · Eosinophilia · Protein malnutrition · Bronchitis/pneumonitis · Children: stunted growth, mental retardation.
What is the Kato-Katz method, and what is its major disadvantage for hookworm diagnosis? +
Kato-Katz is a quantitative fecal examination — uses a metal screen mesh pressed against feces; quantifies intensity of infection in eggs per gram (epg) of stool and estimates worm burden.

Major disadvantage: Rapid clearance of hookworm eggs within 30–60 minutes when glycerine is used as the clearing agent — slides must be read promptly. Direct fecal smear is only useful for heavy infections (>400 epg).
Angiostrongylus cantonensis
What disease does A. cantonensis cause in humans, and why does it cause this instead of a pulmonary infection like in its definitive host? +
Eosinophilic meningitis. In rats (definitive host), larvae complete their cycle by migrating to the brain briefly, then returning to the pulmonary arteries to mature. In humans (accidental host), larvae undergo erratic migration — they get "lost" and cannot complete their normal cycle. They primarily target the brain, causing intense eosinophilic inflammation of the meninges. They may also go to the eyes or lungs.
What are the hosts of A. cantonensis, and what is the infective stage for each? Name three intermediate host species. +
Definitive host: Domestic rats — infective stage: L3 larva
Intermediate host: Mollusks — infective stage inside them: L1 larva (molts to L3 inside)
Accidental host: Humans — infective stage: L3 larva
Paratenic hosts: Crabs, freshwater prawns, lizards, frogs (L3 resides but doesn't develop further)

Three mollusk IH examples: Achatina fulica, Vaginulus plebius, Helicostoma macrostoma.
What is the CSF profile in A. cantonensis infection? What are the Charcot-Leyden crystals and why do they appear? +
CSF profile: Increased WBC (pleocytosis) with eosinophilia making up >10% of WBC — often reaching 90% in A. cantonensis cases (diagnostic threshold for eosinophilic meningitis).

Charcot-Leyden crystals are slender, bipyramidal crystals that are byproducts of eosinophil breakdown. They appear in the CSF as a hallmark of eosinophilic inflammation. Occasional multinucleated giant cells may also appear.
Why is anthelmintic treatment controversial in A. cantonensis infection? What non-drug intervention relieves severe headache dramatically? +
Killing worms in the CNS can theoretically increase the inflammatory reaction, as dying larvae trigger stronger granuloma formation — potentially worsening the patient's condition. Since the disease is usually self-limiting (4–6 weeks), anthelmintics are often unnecessary. When used: albendazole ± corticosteroids.

For severe headache: spinal puncture with removal of ~10 mL of CSF relieves headache dramatically.
Enterobius vermicularis
Describe the egg of Enterobius vermicularis. What is the method of choice for collecting the diagnostic specimen, and when should it be done? +
Egg is D-shaped (asymmetrical) — flattened on one side, convex on the other; 50–60 × 20–30 μm; elongated-ovoidal; colorless; thick double-layered shell (outer triple albuminous covering + inner lipoidal membrane).

Method of choice: Graham's Scotch Adhesive Tape Swab. Press sticky tape firmly on perianal skin → mount sticky side down on a glass slide → add toluene/glycerol (clears debris, makes eggs refractive). Must be collected in the morning BEFORE the patient passes stool or takes a bath (eggs deposited nocturnally).
Why does E. vermicularis deposit eggs on perianal skin rather than inside the bowel? What is retroinfection? +
Eggs require oxygen for final development into the infective stage — inside the anaerobic bowel, they cannot mature. The gravid female migrates nocturnally down the colon, exits through the anus, and deposits all her eggs on the perianal/perineal skin (aerobic environment), then dies. Eggs become infective within a few hours and remain viable up to 13 days.

Retroinfection: eggs hatch in the anal mucosa; larvae emerge and migrate back into the rectum, reinfecting the same host without the egg leaving the body. Life span via retroinfection: 14–18 days.
What distinguishes the adult male from the adult female Enterobius? Which one is rarely seen in practice and why? +
Female (Pinworm): 9–13 mm long · Sharply pointed posterior tail resembling a pin (hence "pinworm") · Vulva opens in mid-third of body · Prominent posterior esophageal bulb (unique to Enterobius).

Male (Seatworm): 2–5 mm long · Strongly curved posterior end · Single copulatory spicule (contrast: Ascaris has 2!) · No gubernaculum.

The male is rarely seen because it dies immediately after copulation and is then eaten by the female for nutrients.
How many swabs are needed with the non-adhesive cellophane anal swab to achieve 90% and 97% sensitivity? When is a patient considered free of infection? +
3 swabs → 90% positive detection
5 swabs → 97% positive detection
6 swabs → very high chance the ovum will be collected
7 consecutive days yielding negative results → patient is considered free of infection.
Ascaris lumbricoides
Describe the four egg types of Ascaris lumbricoides. Which one does NOT float, and why? +
Fertilized: 45–70 × 35–50 μm; brownish (bile-stained); round/oval; trilayered shell (inner vitelline + thick chorionic + outer mammillated albuminous); coarse lecithin granules. Floats.

Unfertilized: 88–94 × 28 μm; narrow and elongated; double-layered (inner chorionic + outer albuminous); vitelline membrane ABSENT → does NOT float (lacks vitelline membrane needed for buoyancy); disorganized refractile mass inside.

Decorticated: Fertilized/unfertilized egg lacking the outer mammillated albuminoid coat → smoother outer layer; developing embryos inside.

Embryonated: Infective stage; coiled L3 embryo inside; 3-layer shell (outer coat may be lost).
How does Ascaris survive digestive juices in the small intestine? What is "Loeffler syndrome"? +
Ascaris survives the SI's digestive environment by secreting pepsin inhibitor 3 and phospholiporin, which neutralize the host's proteolytic enzymes.

Loeffler syndrome (Ascaris pneumonitis) is a type of eosinophilic pneumonia that occurs during larval lung migration — eosinophils accumulate in the lungs in response to larval antigens. It mimics immunity-acquired pneumonia and asthma. Diagnosed by finding larvae in sputum or gastric washings. Occurs most in areas with highly seasonal transmission.
What is "ball of spaghetti" in the context of Ascaris? How many spicules does the adult male have — and why does this matter for board exams? +
"Ball of spaghetti" refers to the spontaneous passing of adult Ascaris worms — when heavily infected, worms gather and twist together in the intestine forming a bolus, which is then expelled as a tangled mass resembling spaghetti.

The adult male Ascaris has 2 spicules. This is a classic boards trap — students confuse it with Enterobius vermicularis which has only 1 spicule. Both are intestinal nematodes, making this distinction a frequent exam question.
Toxocara spp.
Why can fecalysis NOT be used to diagnose toxocariasis in humans? What are the three recommended diagnostic methods? +
Humans are accidental hosts — Toxocara larvae undergo erratic migration through tissues but never mature into egg-laying adults in the human GI tract. Therefore, no eggs are produced or excreted in human feces.

Three diagnostic methods:
1. Tissue biopsy with larval detection — definitive diagnosis
2. ELISA — detects IgG antibodies against Toxocara excretory antigens
3. Western blot — more specific than ELISA but cannot differentiate old vs new infection; used when ELISA is unavailable. Also: TES Antigen Kit and PCR.
Compare the three clinical presentations of toxocariasis. Which age group is affected by each? +
(1) Visceral Larva Migrans (VLM) — mainly young children. Larvae invade multiple tissues (liver, lungs, skeletal muscle, heart). Hypereosinophilia. Features: hepatosplenomegaly, wheezing → pneumonia, lymphadenopathy, myocarditis, fever, weight loss, cough, rashes.

(2) Ocular Larva Migrans (OLM) — older children or young adults. Strabismus ("libat"), ophthalmologic lesions, diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis. Can cause permanent blindness. Visceral manifestations uncommon.

(3) Overt Toxocariasis — any age; chronic. Reddish skin/rash (vasculitis-like), edema of hands, joint pain (mono/polyarthralgia), chronic fatigue, fever, eosinophilia. May lead to encephalitis.
What are the egg differences between T. canis and T. cati? Which species is transmitted more through lactation vs. transplacental route? +
T. canis (dog): Larger — 85 × 75 μm · Thick, corrugated shell · Course and large egg pits · Crater-like egg pits · Subglobose · Dark brown · Spear-shaped cervical alae.

T. cati (cat): Smaller — 65 × 70 μm · Thin corrugated shell · Cave-like egg pits · Subglobose · Superficially pitted · Arrow-shaped cervical alae.

Transmission route:
T. canis → primarily transplacental (puppies) + transmammary
T. cati → vertical transmission attributed more to lactation (less frequent environmental contamination due to cat defecation patterns)

Match each organism to its diagnostic stage: Hookworms · A. cantonensis · E. vermicularis · A. lumbricoides · Toxocara (in humans) +
