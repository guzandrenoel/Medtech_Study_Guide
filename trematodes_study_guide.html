Trematodes — Flukes

Michael David C. Gu, RMT  ·  Second Semester Study Guide
Core identity: Leaf-shaped (except schistosomes), unsegmented, dorsoventrally flat, with an incomplete digestive system (no anus — waste is regurgitated). Most are hermaphroditic. All require a freshwater mollusk as their 1st intermediate host.

Key structures
Oral sucker
Surrounds the mouth at the anterior end. Primary function: nutrition/feeding. Reason flukes are also called "distomes" (two suckers).
Ventral sucker (acetabulum)
Located on ventral surface. Attachment only — holds the worm in position against fluid flow within host organs. Contains no nutritive function.
Tegument
Living acellular/syncytial layer (Neodermata). Absorbs nutrients, excretes waste, and protects against host immunity. May bear spines, papillae, or tubercles.
Flame cells (solenocytes)
Cilia-driven excretory cells. Fan fluid wastes into collecting tubules → excretory bladder → posterior pore. Pattern used taxonomically to identify species.
Genital sucker (gonotyl)
Present only in heterophyids (H. heterophyes). Used during copulation. Located at the left posterior border of the ventral sucker.
Alimentary system
Mouth → muscular pharynx → short esophagus → 2 blind intestinal ceca. No anus — waste is regurgitated. IC are simple in flukes; reunite to form single cecum in schistosomes.
What makes schistosomes unique: Dioecious (separate sexes) · non-operculated eggs · bifid-tailed cercariae · skin penetration route · only 1 intermediate host (snail) · no redia stage · no metacercaria stage · live in "eternal copulation" (gynecophoric canal).

Clinical phases of schistosomiasis
1
Cercarial dermatitis (Swimmer's itch). Pruritic papular rash at penetration site. Self-limiting. Non-human schistosomes cause more severe dermatitis but cannot develop into adults in humans. Also called: clam digger's itch, lake itch, duck itch.
2
Katayama fever (acute schistosomiasis). 4–8 weeks post-infection, at onset of egg-laying. Systemic allergic reaction to massive egg antigen release. Symptoms: fever, chills, cough, rash, myalgia, arthralgia, hepatosplenomegaly, generalized lymphadenopathy, high peripheral eosinophilia. First studied in Japan.
3
Chronic intestinal schistosomiasis (S. mansoni / S. japonicum). Colicky abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea, weight loss. Granulomas in colon/rectum → fibrosis → periportal fibrosis ("clay pipestem fibrosis") → portal hypertension → esophageal varices → ascites.
4
Chronic urinary schistosomiasis (S. haematobium only). Cardinal symptom: terminal hematuria. Dysuria, bladder colic, bladder calcification ("sandy patch" on X-ray). Long-term: hydronephrosis, squamous cell carcinoma of bladder. Eggs passed more at midday.
Immune evasion
Adult worms coat themselves in host proteins — a phenomenon called concomitant immunity (molecular mimicry). This makes them invisible to immune attack for up to 30 years. It is the eggs — not the adult worms — that cause disease, through intense granuloma formation → scarring → fibrosis → organ failure.
Treatment
Praziquantel is the universal DOC for all human schistosomes. Dose: 40–60 mg/kg in divided doses (1 day). Mechanism: increases Ca²⁺ permeability → muscle contraction/paralysis + tegumental damage exposing parasite antigens. Effective against adults only — a second dose weeks later catches worms that were immature at first treatment.
All hermaphroditic liver flukes inhabit the biliary tract/gallbladder. Infection route: ingestion of metacercariae (in fish or aquatic vegetation). Critical exception: Fasciola hepatica requires triclabendazole — praziquantel is INEFFECTIVE.

Fasciola hepatica — special highlights
Praziquantel is INEFFECTIVE against Fasciola.
DOC: Triclabendazole 10 mg/kg single dose. Works against both adult and actively migrating juvenile worms.
Halzoun syndrome (pharyngeal fascioliasis)
Caused by ingestion of raw animal liver infected with adult flukes. Worms use suckers to attach to the pharyngeal mucosa → rapid edema, pain, bleeding, potential respiratory interference, deafness (rare tube blockage), suffocation (rare). Common in Lebanon and Middle East.
Spurious infection — key exam trap
If a patient eats infected liver, eggs pass through their stool without true infection. Always require a 3-day liver-free diet before repeat stool testing. Eggs don't appear until 3–4 months after infection anyway.
Migration path
Metacercariae excyst in duodenum → pierce intestinal wall → peritoneal cavity → crawl to liver → Glisson's capsule → liver parenchyma → biliary passages. Spends ~2 months tunneling through liver parenchyma. Matures in ~3–4 months.
Dicrocoelium dendriticum — the brainworm
Unique terrestrial life cycle — no water required.
Eggs → feces of sheep → ingested by land snails → cercariae form in slime balls secreted by snail → eaten by ants (genus Formica) → most metacercariae encyst in ant's body, but one ("the brainworm") migrates to the subesophageal ganglion → behavioral manipulation: infected ant climbs blade of grass at night and clamps its mandibles — perfectly positioned to be eaten by grazing sheep next morning → sheep (definitive host) ingests infected ant while grazing.

Lobed testes are ANTERIOR to the ovary (opposite of Clonorchis/Opisthorchis). Human infections usually spurious (from eating infected sheep liver).
All intestinal flukes are hermaphroditic, transmitted by metacercariae ingestion, and require a freshwater snail as 1st IH. Treatment: praziquantel for all.

Heterophyids — ectopic danger
Heterophyid myocarditis: Eggs of H. heterophyes / M. yokogawai can invade intestinal mucosa → enter lymphatics/circulation → embolize to heart, brain, spinal cord. Myocarditis leading to heart failure reported in 14–15% of Philippine cases. Most dangerous destination is the myocardium — trapped eggs provoke severe granulomatous reaction.
E. ilocanum — Philippine highlights
2nd IH is another snail (Pila luzonica/Pila conica) — unique among intestinal flukes. Common where raw kuhol are eaten with lime or coconut milk. Discovered by Garrison in 1907 in Manila. Collar of 49–51 spines around oral sucker is the key distinguishing feature.
P. westermani — Oriental lung fluke. ~20 million infected worldwide. Often misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis. Adults found in pairs within fibrotic capsules in lung parenchyma.

Clinical features
Pulmonary (chronic) phase
Fibrotic cysts form around adult worm pairs. Chronic cough (most common symptom) · hemoptysis (bloodstained/"rusty" sputum) · chest pain · dyspnea.

TB mimicry — frequently misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis. Differentiate by: eggs in sputum, serology (ELISA up to 99% sensitive), CT showing "ring shadows."
Ectopic paragonimiasis — cerebral
Route: worms migrate through soft tissues around the jugular vein into the skull. Most common in children.

Symptoms: seizures (Jackson epilepsy), severe headache, visual disturbances, hemiplegia. Prognosis: often serious or fatal if untreated early.
General digenean life cycle — step by step
1
Eggs exit definitive host via feces, urine (S. haematobium), or sputum (P. westermani) and reach water — or are ingested directly by 1st IH (C. sinensis, Opisthorchis).
2
Miracidium — free-swimming, fully ciliated 1st stage larva. Lifespan: 24–48 hours. Does not feed. Has specialized glands to bore into snail tissue. Must find its snail quickly or die.
3
Sporocyst — sac-like, contains germ balls. Asexual multiplication begins. Schistosomes produce a 2nd generation sporocyst here; all other flukes proceed to redia.
4
Redia — more complex larva with pharynx and gut. Produced within sporocyst. Absent in schistosomes. A single miracidium can produce over 500,000 cercariae.
5
Cercariae — final larval stage in snail host. Emerge into water. Schistosomes: bifid (forked) tails — directly penetrate human skin. Others: straight/tapered tail — encyst as metacercariae.
6
Metacercariae — encysted resting stage (absent in schistosomes). Found on aquatic vegetation or inside tissues of 2nd IH (fish, crab, another snail). Human infection via ingestion.
7
Adult worm matures in definitive host (excystation occurs in duodenum), migrates to final habitat, copulates, and produces eggs to restart the cycle.

Diagnostic techniques
Direct saline wet mount
Simplest method. Requires 400× magnification for very small eggs (e.g., Clonorchis at 28–35 μm).
Kato-Katz method
Quantifies eggs per gram of stool → estimates total worm burden. Correlates with disease severity. Used in field studies and to monitor chemotherapy. Superior to flotation for Heterophyids.
Formalin-ether (FECT) concentration
Best for light infections. Critical note: trematode eggs do NOT float — always use sedimentation techniques, never flotation.
Hatching test
Place eggs in warm fresh water; watch for miracidia to hatch. Used to check viability of eggs — if miracidia hatch, infection is active.
Serology — ELISA / IFA / Immunoblot
Useful for early/light infections, travelers, ectopic disease. Fasciola ELISA becomes positive within 2 weeks and goes negative after treatment. Paragonimiasis ELISA: up to 99% sensitivity.
Imaging
Ultrasound: liver fibrosis, dilated bile ducts, bladder thickening. CT: liver/lung cysts, brain lesions (P. westermani ring shadows). ERCP: occluded bile ducts (C. sinensis). Chest X-ray: ring shadows for paragonimiasis.

High-yield questions
What is the primary difference between schistosomes and all other trematodes? +
Schistosomes are dioecious (separate sexes), have only one intermediate host (snail), lack the redia stage and metacercariae stage, produce non-operculated eggs, and their bifid-tailed cercariae directly penetrate unbroken human skin — instead of being ingested as metacercariae. They also live in venous plexuses rather than organ lumens.
Why is praziquantel ineffective against Fasciola hepatica? What should be used instead? +
Fasciola has tegumental resistance to praziquantel's mechanism (Ca²⁺ permeability increase + tegumental damage). DOC: triclabendazole 10 mg/kg single dose — the only drug effective against both adult and actively migrating juvenile worms. Alternative: bithionol (more toxic). Remember: this is a major boards trap.
Which schistosome produces the most eggs per day, and why is this clinically significant? +
S. japonicum — up to 3,500 eggs/pair/day (vs ~300 for S. haematobium). Because so many eggs are produced, a high percentage enters general circulation, causing severe pathology in the liver, lungs, and even the brain. Its smooth integument, high egg output, and lack of host specificity (infects virtually all mammals) make it the hardest schistosome to control.
What is Katayama fever? Which species most commonly causes it and why? +
Katayama fever is an acute serum sickness-like syndrome occurring 4–8 weeks post-infection, at the onset of egg-laying. Caused by a massive systemic allergic reaction to egg antigens. Symptoms: fever, chills, cough, rash, myalgia, arthralgia, hepatosplenomegaly, generalized lymphadenopathy, high peripheral eosinophilia. Named after a Japanese district. Most severe with S. japonicum due to its much higher egg output. Also occurs with S. mansoni.
What is the cardinal symptom of S. haematobium? Why does it occur at a specific time? +
Terminal hematuria — blood at the end of urination. Eggs pierce the vesical wall using their terminal spine and lytic enzymes, releasing blood into the bladder lumen. Eggs are passed more abundantly at midday — possibly an adaptation coinciding with when humans are most likely to be in water, improving chances of miracidial transmission to the snail intermediate host.
Describe the "brainworm" phenomenon of Dicrocoelium dendriticum. +
Most cercariae encyst in the ant's body as metacercariae, but one cercariae (the "brainworm") migrates to the subesophageal ganglion of the ant. This causes behavioral manipulation: the infected ant climbs to the tip of a blade of grass at night and clamps its mandibles — perfectly positioning itself to be eaten by grazing sheep the next morning. This is the only trematode with a fully terrestrial life cycle (no water required). 2nd IH = ants (genus Formica).
What is the most dangerous ectopic complication of Heterophyes heterophyes in the Philippines? +
Heterophyid myocarditis — eggs invade the intestinal mucosa and embolize via lymphatics/circulation to the myocardium, causing severe granulomatous reactions and heart failure. Reported in 14–15% of Philippine cases. Despite its minute size (<2 mm), H. heterophyes can be fatal through this ectopic complication. Eggs can also reach the brain and spinal cord.
Why is P. westermani frequently misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis? +
Both cause chronic cough, hemoptysis (bloodstained/rusty sputum), chest pain, and pulmonary infiltrates on X-ray. Differentiate by: identifying large operculated, yellowish-brown eggs in sputum or feces; serology (ELISA up to 99% sensitive); CT showing "ring shadows" (cysts) rather than TB consolidation/cavitation. P. westermani eggs are unembryonated when freshly laid — a key feature.
C. sinensis vs O. viverrini — key morphological and clinical differentiators? +
Testes shape is the key: C. sinensis has 4 highly branched (dendritic) testes in the posterior third. O. viverrini has lobed (NOT branched) testes arranged obliquely. Both cause cholangiocarcinoma — but sinensis in China/Korea/Japan, viverrini in NE Thailand (up to 70% of some Thai villages). Viverrini has lesser opercular shoulders and may show "melon-like" ridges on eggs under high magnification.
What is the intermediate host rule that applies universally to ALL trematodes? +
The 1st intermediate host is ALWAYS a freshwater mollusk (snail) — no exceptions across any trematode species. Asexual multiplication occurs in the snail, and the magnitude is enormous: a single miracidium can give rise to over half a million cercariae. This compensates for the extremely low probability that any single egg in nature will find a suitable host.
What does "concomitant immunity" mean in schistosomiasis, and what is its clinical implication? +
Adult schistosomes coat their tegument with host proteins (molecular mimicry), making them functionally invisible to the immune system — they can survive for up to 30 years. Clinical implication: the adult worms cause little damage. Disease is caused by the host's intense inflammatory (granulomatous) reaction to the thousands of trapped eggs in tissues (liver, bladder) → granuloma formation → scarring → fibrosis → portal hypertension or bladder carcinoma.
